A legislative ledger · 1945–2026

It wasn't the avocado toast.
It was a paper trail.

Between 1945 and 2026, Congress, administrations of both parties, and a line of Supreme Court decisions rewrote the rules that once split America's productivity gains with the people who produced them. Here is what was passed, when, by whom, and with whose votes.

I. The two eras

Then: 1945–1979

Productivity and pay moved together. When American workers produced more, they were paid more — not out of corporate generosity, but because the tax code, labor law, and antitrust enforcement of that period made it the path of least resistance. More than a third of private-sector workers carried a union card at the 1945 peak, and still roughly a quarter at the end of the 1970s. The top marginal tax rate never fell below 70%. One income bought a house.

Now: 1979–2026

The two lines separated and never rejoined. Since 1979, worker productivity has grown 93.2%. Pay for the typical worker has grown 33.7%. That gap is not a rounding error or a statistical artifact — the 59-point spread is roughly two-thirds of all the productivity growth American workers generated over two generations, and it went somewhere other than their paychecks.

The question this document answers: the money didn't vanish. It was redirected — into corporate profits, share buybacks, executive compensation, and private fortunes. That redirection required changes to the law. Those changes have dates, sponsors, roll-call votes, and signatures. What follows is the receipt.

II. Pillar A — Rewriting the tax code

The mechanism is simple: lower the top rates, and tax money made from owning things at a lower rate than money made from working. Over forty years the top marginal income tax rate fell from 91% to 37%, and the long-term capital gains rate fell to roughly half the top wage rate — which is why a billionaire whose income arrives as appreciated stock can face a lower effective rate than the nurse who cleans up after him.

Note the reversal, because it is the whole argument in one fact. From 1972 to 1981, the tax code capped the rate on earned income — wages and salary — at 50%, while unearned income faced 70%. Working was taxed less than owning. Today it is precisely inverted: the top rate on wages is 37%, and the top rate on long-term capital gains is 20%. The code did not drift. It was turned around.

The carried interest loophole: four chances to close it, four survivals

Carried interest lets private equity and hedge fund managers treat their fee income as capital gains, taxed at roughly half the rate of the wages earned by the people who work at the companies they buy. It survived the 1986 reform. It survived the 2017 TCJA, which merely stretched the required holding period from one year to three. It survived the 2021 Build Back Better negotiations, and in August 2022 the provision that would have narrowed it was stripped from the Inflation Reduction Act at the demand of a single senator — Kyrsten Sinema (D-AZ) — as the price of her vote.

III. Pillar B — Breaking labor's bargaining power

A worker's wage is set by leverage. Every mechanism that gave American workers leverage — sectoral unions, the strike, a wage floor indexed to living costs, the right to sue as a group — has been narrowed, one statute and one court decision at a time.

The result: private-sector union membership fell from roughly one in three workers in the mid-1950s to 5.9% in 2025. And the federal minimum wage — $7.25 — has now been frozen across four presidential terms and nine Congresses. Refusing to act for seventeen consecutive years is not a failure to legislate. It is a legislative outcome.

IV. Pillar C — Financialization and the end of antitrust

1982: the year buying your own stock stopped being manipulation

Before November 1982, a corporation buying its own shares on the open market exposed itself to manipulation liability under the securities laws, and companies largely stayed away. Then SEC Rule 10b-18 created a safe harbor: follow four conditions on timing, price, volume and broker, and the SEC would not treat the repurchase as manipulation. This was not an act of Congress — it was a rule adopted by a commission whose chairman, John Shad, had been appointed by Ronald Reagan (R) the year before. Shad came to the job as vice chairman of E.F. Hutton, the first Wall Street executive to run the SEC since 1935, and had chaired Reagan's New York fundraising.

In 2025, S&P 500 companies spent roughly $1 trillion buying back their own shares — a record, and more than $777 billion of it confirmed in the first three quarters alone. That is capital that did not go to wages, prices, or domestic investment. It went to shareholders — and the wealthiest 10% of households own the overwhelming majority of American stock. Combined with the 1993 §162(m) loophole that pushed executive pay into options and shares, this created a direct incentive for management to route company cash into the share price rather than the payroll.

V. Pillar D — Money in politics

This is the pillar that holds up the other three. Each of the changes above had to survive an election. The rules governing whether they could be punished at the ballot box were rewritten mostly by courts — which means the relevant question is not who voted, but who appointed.

Congress's role here was largely one of omission. It passed McCain-Feingold in 2002; after Citizens United gutted it, no replacement disclosure regime — the DISCLOSE Act and its successors — has ever cleared the Senate. And Congress has never meaningfully restricted its own members from trading the stocks of the industries they regulate.

VI. The scoreboard

If you want this argument to survive contact with someone who knows the history, do not tell it as a story about one party. Tell it accurately, because accurate is more damning:

Where Republicans own it outright

  • Every top-rate cut since 1980 — 1981, 1986, 2001, 2003, 2017 — was signed by a Republican president, and the 2025 bill made the lowest of them permanent. (The one big exception runs the other way in time, not in party: the 91%-to-70% cut of 1964 was Johnson's.)
  • The 2017 corporate cut from 35% to 21% and the 2025 permanence bill both passed by reconciliation with zero Democratic votes.
  • Taft-Hartley, over a Democratic president's veto, by a Republican Congress.
  • Rule 10b-18, the PATCO firings, and the antitrust retreat — all Reagan-era executive and regulatory acts.
  • Every justice in the Citizens United, McCutcheon, Janus, and Epic Systems majorities was appointed by a Republican president.

Where Democrats are implicated

  • Bill Clinton signed all three of the biggest financial-deregulation and capital-gains bills — the 1997 capital gains cut, Gramm-Leach-Bliley in 1999, and the derivatives exemption in 2000 — each passed by a Republican Congress, each with large bipartisan majorities. Gramm-Leach-Bliley cleared the Senate 90–8.
  • §162(m), the executive-pay provision that supercharged stock-option compensation, was a 1993 Democratic bill signed by a Democratic president.
  • Labor law reform failed in 1978 and 2009–10 under Democratic trifectas. The Employee Free Choice Act, having already died to a filibuster in 2007, never got a floor vote in either chamber once Democrats actually held both.
  • Sixteen Senate Democrats supplied the margin to roll back Dodd-Frank in 2018.
  • The 2022 carried-interest fix was killed by a Democratic senator.

The honest conclusion

One party drove. The other, often enough, declined to take the wheel — and sometimes held the door. The consistent through-line across eight decades is not Republican versus Democrat. It is capital versus labor, and capital has an unbroken winning streak because it funds both campaigns.

That is why the argument matters more than the partisanship. The people telling you that your problem is DoorDash and iced coffee are not confused about the data. They are pointing at your grocery receipt so you don't read the roll call.

VII. The sixty-second version

If you only have a minute, this is the whole thing:

  1. From 1945 to 1979, pay tracked productivity. Since 1979, productivity is up 93% and typical pay is up 34%. That gap is the theft, and it's measurable.
  2. Congress cut the top tax rate from 91% to 37%, and taxed money made from owning at roughly half the rate of money made from working.
  3. Congress froze the minimum wage at $7.25 for seventeen years and let every labor-law reform die — including under its own majorities.
  4. Reagan's SEC legalized stock buybacks in 1982. Last year they hit a trillion dollars — money that could have been wages.
  5. Congress repealed Glass-Steagall in 1999 and exempted derivatives in 2000. The 2008 crash followed. Congress bailed out the banks, not the ten million families who lost their homes. One banker went to prison. He was a managing director at Credit Suisse, not the CEO of anything.
  6. Five Republican-appointed justices then made it legal for the winners of steps 2 through 5 to spend unlimited money keeping steps 2 through 5 in place.

And in June 2026, one man became worth a trillion dollars. If you had earned a million dollars a day, every day, since the birth of Christ, you still wouldn't have that much. He did not get there by skipping brunch, and nobody is broke because they didn't.

VIII. What they will hit you with

Every claim above is sourced, but four counterarguments come up constantly. Know them cold, because being caught flat-footed on a technicality is how a true argument loses a room.

"The productivity–pay gap disappears if you count benefits."

It shrinks; it does not disappear. Total compensation including employer-paid health premiums has grown faster than wages alone. But that is not the defense it sounds like: it means a large share of the raise American workers earned was absorbed by health insurance premiums rising far faster than inflation. The money went to a hospital system and an insurer instead of a bank account. Workers still cannot spend it on rent.

"Housing is a supply and zoning problem, not a billionaire problem."

Supply is real and it is a genuine constraint — the country underbuilt for fifteen years after 2008. But zoning is written by people who run for office, and the same tax treatment that makes housing an attractive store of wealth for capital is federal law. Concede the supply point and keep going: restricted supply and financialized demand are the same story told from two ends.

"The 1964 tax cut was a Democrat, and Clinton signed the deregulation."

Correct on both counts, which is why they are in the scoreboard above. This argument is not improved by pretending one party is clean. It is improved by pointing out that capital won under both.

"Living standards are objectively higher — people have more stuff."

True, and irrelevant to the claim. Consumer electronics got cheaper because of global manufacturing and Moore's law. The four things that determine whether a life is stable — housing, healthcare, higher education, and childcare — all got dramatically more expensive relative to income over the same period. A flat-screen television is cheap. A place to put it is not.
